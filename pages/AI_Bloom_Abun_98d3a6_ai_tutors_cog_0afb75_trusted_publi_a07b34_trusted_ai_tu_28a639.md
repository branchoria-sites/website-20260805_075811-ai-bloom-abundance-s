## Introduction

If AI tutors are to become trusted public infrastructure for lifelong learning, they should not simply receive a one-off approval badge. They should meet a continuously monitored certification system that tests educational quality, safety, transparency, privacy, accessibility and accountability throughout their working lives. Unlike a textbook, an AI tutor generates new responses every day, may be updated frequently and can change behaviour as underlying models evolve. A trustworthy certification system therefore needs to evaluate both the organisation running the tutor and the tutor's real-world performance over time.

This matters well beyond today's classrooms. If AI is to support broader [human]({{ 'human-feedback/' | relative_url }}) flourishing by making high-quality learning available throughout life, people must be able to trust that their tutor helps them learn accurately, protects their interests and can be held accountable when it fails. Certification is not about guaranteeing perfection; it is about creating reliable public confidence through clear standards, independent oversight and continuous improvement rather than marketing claims alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=84636" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">guidance generative ai education and research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guidance for generative AI in education and research &#124; UNESCOSeptember 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 7, 2023</span></span></span>

## What certification should test

A credible certification scheme should examine the complete learning experience rather than only the technical performance of the AI model.

The most important areas include:

* **Educational effectiveness.** The tutor should demonstrate that it supports genuine learning rather than merely producing answers. Certification should examine whether it encourages reasoning, retrieval practice, reflection and appropriate feedback instead of becoming a shortcut for completing assignments. Educational experts—not only AI engineers—should participate in these evaluations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2023/12/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2023_c827b81a/full-report/opportunities-guidelines-and-guardrails-for-effective-and-equitable-use-of-ai-in-education_2f0862dc.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oecd.org">[oecd.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oecd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">opportunities guidelines and guardrails for effective and equitable use of ai in education 2f0862dc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Opportunities, guidelines and guardrails for effective and equitable use of AI in education: OECD Digital Education Outlook 2023 &#124; OECDDe...</span></span></span>
* **Accuracy and source transparency.** Users should be able to distinguish established knowledge from uncertainty. The tutor should cite reliable sources where appropriate, indicate confidence levels when evidence is mixed and avoid inventing references. Independent testing should measure factual reliability across different subjects and learner groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=84636" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">guidance generative ai education and research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guidance for generative AI in education and research &#124; UNESCOSeptember 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 7, 2023</span></span></span>
* **Protection of learner data.** Educational conversations often reveal sensitive information about ability, interests, disabilities or career ambitions. Certification should verify that personal data are collected only when necessary, securely stored, clearly governed and not quietly repurposed for advertising or unrelated commercial uses without appropriate safeguards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=84636" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">guidance generative ai education and research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guidance for generative AI in education and research &#124; UNESCOSeptember 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 7, 2023</span></span></span>
* **Accessibility and inclusion.** Trusted tutors should work for learners with disabilities, support multiple languages where possible and avoid systematic bias against particular groups. Testing should include diverse users rather than assuming that one benchmark represents everyone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=84636" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">guidance generative ai education and research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guidance for generative AI in education and research &#124; UNESCOSeptember 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 7, 2023</span></span></span>
* **Human oversight.** Certification should ensure that users know when to seek a teacher, librarian or other qualified professional. AI tutors should recognise their [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}) instead of presenting uncertain answers with unwarranted confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=84636" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">guidance generative ai education and research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guidance for generative AI in education and research &#124; UNESCOSeptember 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 7, 2023</span></span></span>
* **Resilience against misuse.** Systems should be evaluated for prompt injection, manipulation, inappropriate content generation and other foreseeable failures. A trustworthy educational assistant should remain dependable even when users deliberately or accidentally push it beyond its intended role.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/artificial-intelligence/ai-standards" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AI Standards &#124; NIST</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AI Standards &#124; NIST...</span></span></span>

Together, these requirements would move certification beyond a narrow software test towards an assessment of whether the tutor genuinely serves learners and the public interest.

## Why continuous evaluation is necessary

Traditional educational accreditation often assumes that a textbook or curriculum remains relatively stable for years. AI tutors are different. Their underlying models may be updated weekly or even daily, changing their behaviour without users noticing.

This means certification cannot be a one-time event. Instead, it should resemble aviation safety or pharmaceutical monitoring, combining initial approval with ongoing surveillance.

A continuous system could include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* routine re-testing after major model updates;
* public reporting of important failures and corrective actions;
* monitoring for changes in accuracy, bias or educational performance;
* mechanisms allowing teachers and learners to report problems;
* periodic independent audits rather than relying solely on company self-assessment.

</div>

This lifecycle approach is increasingly reflected in international [AI governance]({{ 'ai-governance/' | relative_url }}) frameworks. ISO/IEC 42001, the first international AI management system standard, emphasises continual improvement rather than static compliance. Similarly, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework treats trustworthy AI as an ongoing process of governing, measuring and managing risks instead of a one-off certification exercise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.iso.org/standard/42001?gad_source=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iso.org">[iso.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iso.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISO/IEC 42001:2023 - AI management systemsAugust 23, 1970...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 23, 1970</span></span></span>

For conversational tutors, continuous evaluation also creates incentives for developers to improve educational quality over time instead of optimising only for product launch.

## Who should audit and enforce the standards?

No single organisation is likely to command sufficient public trust on its own.

A stronger model would divide responsibilities among several institutions.

**Independent educational experts** should assess whether tutoring supports genuine learning rather than superficial task completion.

**Technical auditors** should evaluate cybersecurity, robustness, privacy protections, testing procedures and model governance.

**Accessibility specialists** should verify that disabled learners and speakers of different languages are not systematically disadvantaged.

**Public regulators** should establish minimum legal requirements, particularly where AI tutors are used in schools, universities or publicly funded lifelong learning programmes.

**Accredited third-party certification bodies** could perform recurring audits in a manner similar to existing certification systems for information security, quality management or medical devices.

This separation reduces conflicts of interest. Developers may perform extensive internal testing, but independent assessment provides greater public confidence that certification reflects genuine performance rather than commercial incentives. UNESCO has similarly argued for regulatory frameworks that protect learners while maintaining human oversight, and recent OECD guidance stresses that accountability for educational decisions must remain clearly assigned to institutions and professionals rather than delegated to AI systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=84636" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">guidance generative ai education and research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guidance for generative AI in education and research &#124; UNESCOSeptember 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 7, 2023</span></span></span>

## Existing standards provide building blocks, not a complete solution

No widely adopted certification exists today that fully certifies an AI tutor's educational trustworthiness.

However, several international frameworks already provide useful foundations.

ISO/IEC 42001 offers requirements for organisational AI governance, including risk management, documentation and continual improvement. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides methods for identifying, measuring and managing trustworthy AI characteristics such as validity, safety, explainability and accountability. UNESCO's guidance for generative AI in [education]({{ 'education/' | relative_url }}) focuses specifically on educational settings, highlighting learner protection, privacy, transparency and human-centred design. OECD work on AI in education and vocational training similarly emphasises accountability, transparency, inclusiveness and secure data governance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.iso.org/standard/42001?gad_source=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iso.org">[iso.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iso.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISO/IEC 42001:2023 - AI management systemsAugust 23, 1970...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 23, 1970</span></span></span>

None of these frameworks alone certifies whether an AI tutor is a good teacher. Instead, they suggest that future educational certification should combine governance standards with educational quality assurance and ongoing classroom evidence.

## What a public certification mark could mean

A recognisable public certification could eventually function much like food hygiene ratings, energy efficiency labels or accessibility certifications: not a guarantee that every interaction will be flawless, but an easily understood signal that minimum standards have been independently verified.

A certified AI tutor might therefore be expected to publish:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* what subjects and age groups it has been evaluated for;
* how frequently it is re-tested;
* known limitations and confidence boundaries;
* how learner data are handled;
* how users can challenge incorrect or harmful outputs;
* the date of the most recent independent audit.

</div>

Making these details public would allow schools, libraries, employers and individual learners to compare systems on more than marketing claims alone.

## Why certification matters for lifelong learning

The optimistic vision behind trusted public AI tutors is not simply that everyone will have access to conversational software. It is that reliable educational assistance could become a permanent part of civic infrastructure, helping people learn throughout changing careers, longer lives and rapidly evolving scientific knowledge.

That ambition depends on trust. If learners cannot distinguish between well-governed educational assistants and poorly supervised systems, confidence in AI-supported learning may erode, even where the technology is genuinely beneficial.

Certification therefore serves a broader purpose than consumer protection. It helps create the institutional foundations needed for AI tutors to become dependable public resources that expand access to knowledge while protecting learners' rights. In the larger vision of AI-enabled human flourishing, trustworthy certification is one of the governance mechanisms that could make abundant, lifelong learning both widely available and worthy of public confidence.
