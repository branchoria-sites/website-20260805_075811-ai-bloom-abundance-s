## Introduction

If advanced AI is to become a genuine engine of lifelong learning rather than another premium service, one question becomes central: who should provide trustworthy AI tutors? The technical ability to build capable educational assistants is only part of the challenge. Equally important is whether everyone can access reliable, well-governed tutoring regardless of income, age or location.

Within the broader vision of AI enabling long-term [human]({{ 'human-feedback/' | relative_url }}) flourishing, trusted public AI tutors could become part of society's educational infrastructure, much as libraries, public schools and universities already are. Rather than leaving lifelong learning entirely to commercial subscriptions, governments, colleges, libraries and employment services could help ensure that high-quality AI guidance remains broadly available, accountable and aligned with educational goals. Whether that vision succeeds depends less on AI capability alone than on governance, public trust and institutional design.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=67098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">guidance generative ai education and research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guidance for generative AI in education and research &#124; UNESCOSeptember 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 7, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why access quality may diverge

Today's AI tutors already vary enormously in quality. Some are carefully designed around established learning science, encouraging learners to reason through problems, identify misconceptions and practise retrieval. Others function mainly as conversational assistants that answer questions quickly but are less [effective]({{ 'tutor-design/' | relative_url }}) at promoting durable understanding.

This creates the risk of a two-tier educational system. Wealthier learners may gain access to premium tutors trained on carefully curated educational content, [personalised]({{ 'influence-loops/' | relative_url }}) learning histories and advanced reasoning models, while others rely on free services with weaker safeguards, intrusive advertising or lower educational quality.

## Why public institutions may become trusted providers

[Education]({{ 'education/' | relative_url }}) has traditionally been treated as a public good because societies benefit when knowledge is widely shared. The same logic may increasingly apply to AI tutoring.

Public institutions already possess assets that commercial providers often lack:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* established educational expertise;
* public accountability;
* existing relationships with learners;
* curriculum knowledge;
* legal responsibilities around privacy and equality;
* long-term incentives that extend beyond subscription revenue.

</div>

Libraries illustrate this particularly well. For generations they have provided trusted access to books, reference services, digital resources and community learning without requiring users to purchase everything individually. AI tutors could become a natural extension of that role, helping citizens navigate increasingly complex knowledge throughout their lives.

Similarly, community colleges, universities, adult education providers and employment services already help people retrain during economic transitions. As AI changes labour markets, these institutions may increasingly provide [trusted AI]({{ 'tutor-standards/' | relative_url }}) learning assistants alongside human advisers, allowing workers to acquire new skills repeatedly across decades rather than relying on one-off qualifications.

Rather than replacing teachers or librarians, AI could multiply their reach by providing continuous support between human interactions while remaining embedded within accountable educational institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-unicef-and-itu-launch-charter-public-digital-learning-platforms?hub=701" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UNESC O, UNICEF and ITU launch Charter for Public Digital Learning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO, UNICEF and ITU launch Charter for Public Digital LearningMarch 19, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 19, 2026</span></span></span>

## Public AI tutors as educational infrastructure

Thinking of AI tutors as infrastructure changes the policy discussion.

Roads, public health systems and libraries are not expected to maximise shareholder returns. They exist because broad access creates benefits that spread throughout society. Knowledge infrastructure may deserve similar treatment if AI tutoring becomes an essential gateway to education, employment and civic participation.

Within the broader AI Bloom perspective, this matters because abundant intelligence only contributes to widespread human flourishing if people can actually reach it.

Potential public benefits include:

* helping adults retrain after technological disruption;
* supporting people returning to education later in life;
* reducing educational inequality between regions;
* assisting migrants through multilingual tutoring;
* providing [accessible]({{ 'accessible-tutors/' | relative_url }}) learning for disabled users;
* supporting older adults who wish to continue learning;
* offering educational continuity during unemployment or career changes.

The economic gains from higher productivity are important, but the wider objective is expanding human capability over an entire lifetime.

## Certification matters as much as capability

If public institutions recommend or provide AI tutors, citizens need reasons to trust them.

Unlike traditional textbooks, conversational AI generates new responses continuously. That makes quality assurance an ongoing process rather than a one-time review.

Useful certification could include requirements such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* transparent identification that users are interacting with AI;
* evidence that educational claims are grounded in reliable sources;
* regular evaluation against recognised educational standards;
* documented testing for hallucinations and misleading explanations;
* clear privacy protections for learner data;
* accessibility standards;
* independent auditing;
* mechanisms for reporting and correcting failures.

</div>

The goal is not to certify that an AI is always correct—a standard no human teacher could meet—but to demonstrate that providers meet recognised standards for educational quality, transparency and accountability.

## Accountability cannot disappear behind the algorithm

One temptation is to imagine AI tutors replacing institutional responsibility. Most education policy points in the opposite direction.

If an AI gives poor advice, reinforces misconceptions or mishandles sensitive personal information, responsibility must remain with identifiable organisations rather than disappearing into opaque technology.

This means institutions need clear answers to practical questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Who selected the model?
* Who approves educational content?
* Who investigates complaints?
* Who monitors accuracy over time?
* Who protects learner records?
* Who decides when human educators should intervene?

</div>

Recent OECD guidance on AI in vocational education argues that AI should strengthen rather than weaken institutional accountability. Organisations remain responsible for educational quality even when AI contributes to curriculum [development]({{ 'build-rights/' | relative_url }}) or learner support.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/five-principles-for-the-effective-use-of-ai-in-vocational-education-and-training-development_ee8c8e8e-en/full-report.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oecd.org">[oecd.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oecd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">full report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Five principles for the effective use of AI in vocational education and training development &#124; OECDJune 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## Public trust depends on human oversight

Evidence from educational AI increasingly suggests that the strongest outcomes come from combining AI with human expertise rather than replacing it.

Human oversight serves several functions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* correcting factual mistakes;
* recognising emotional or safeguarding concerns;
* adapting advice to local circumstances;
* identifying when learners need professional support;
* ensuring educational goals remain central.

</div>

Research into human-AI tutoring systems has found that AI can help less-experienced tutors adopt stronger pedagogical practices while leaving final judgement with humans. Other recent studies suggest that purpose-built educational AI can support learning effectively when carefully supervised and designed around educational objectives rather than unrestricted conversation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03017" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Tutor Co Pilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time ExpertiseOctober 3, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 3, 2024</span></span></span>

## Risks if trusted access is neglected

If governments simply leave AI tutoring entirely to market forces, several long-term risks emerge.

First, educational quality may increasingly depend on ability to pay for better models.

Second, commercial incentives may favour engagement over learning, encouraging systems that keep users interacting rather than gradually making them more independent.

Third, extensive learner data could become concentrated within a small number of technology companies, raising concerns about privacy and market power.

Finally, educational content itself could become fragmented across incompatible proprietary ecosystems, making it harder for public institutions to maintain common standards or guarantee equal opportunity.

None of these outcomes is inevitable, but each becomes more plausible if trusted public alternatives fail to develop alongside commercial offerings.

## A governance choice that shapes AI Bloom

The optimistic case for AI-assisted lifelong learning is not simply that intelligent tutors become technically impressive. It is that reliable educational guidance becomes as widely available as public schooling or libraries once made literacy.

That outcome depends on institutions as much as algorithms. Trusted public AI tutors would require investment, independent evaluation, transparent governance and sustained human oversight. If those conditions are met, AI could help make high-quality personalised education a broadly shared capability rather than a luxury, supporting repeated learning across an entire lifetime and enabling more people to participate in the larger possibilities of scientific progress, economic adaptation and long-term human flourishing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-unicef-and-itu-launch-charter-public-digital-learning-platforms?hub=701" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UNESC O, UNICEF and ITU launch Charter for Public Digital Learning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO, UNICEF and ITU launch Charter for Public Digital LearningMarch 19, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 19, 2026</span></span></span>
