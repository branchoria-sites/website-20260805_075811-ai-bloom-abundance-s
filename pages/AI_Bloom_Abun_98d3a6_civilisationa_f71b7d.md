## Introduction

Could AI make civilisation safer and larger? The strongest case is not that artificial intelligence will remove every danger, but that it could expand humanity’s ability to prevent disasters, recover from shocks and extend the reach of intelligent life over very long timescales. Advanced AI could help detect pandemics earlier, design stronger infrastructure, improve scientific understanding of threats and support better coordination between societies. At the same time, the same capabilities could introduce new vulnerabilities: cyber conflict, misinformation, dangerous biological assistance, concentration of power and loss of [human]({{ 'human-feedback/' | relative_url }}) control over increasingly capable systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">28, 2025...</span></span></span>

Within the wider vision of an AI bloom, civilisational resilience is the question of whether greater intelligence can help humanity move from a fragile species dependent on one planet and imperfect institutions towards a more durable, adaptable and expansive civilisation. The optimistic possibility is not simply survival. It is the preservation and enlargement of humanity’s long future: more generations able to live, create, discover and experience the universe.

## Why resilience becomes the central AI question

Human civilisation has always faced catastrophic risks, from pandemics and asteroid impacts to wars, ecological disruption and technological accidents. Modern civilisation is more capable than previous societies, but it is also more interconnected and therefore exposed to failures that can spread globally. Researchers studying global catastrophic risks have highlighted that advanced technology can create both new hazards and new tools for managing them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalshieldpolicy.org/report/policy-brief-how-governments-can-better-understand-global-catastrophic-risk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalshieldpolicy.org">[Global Shield]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalshieldpolicy.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Global Shield How governments can better understand global catastrophic risk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global Shield How governments can better understand global catastrophic risk</span></span></span>

AI changes this balance because intelligence itself is becoming a technological resource. A society with powerful AI systems could potentially analyse complex threats faster, simulate possible futures, discover solutions and coordinate responses across institutions. The same capability could also amplify mistakes if deployed without adequate safeguards. The long-term question is therefore not whether AI is powerful, but whether that power increases civilisation’s ability to remain stable, adaptable and aligned with human flourishing.

A resilient civilisation would not merely avoid extinction. It would develop the capacity to absorb shocks while continuing to improve. That includes:

* detecting emerging threats before they become crises;
* maintaining essential infrastructure during disasters;
* accelerating scientific responses to disease, climate and environmental challenges;
* reducing dependence on single points of failure;
* creating institutions capable of managing increasingly powerful technologies.

## Pandemic, disaster and infrastructure resilience

### Could AI help humanity respond before crises escalate?

Pandemics show the importance of speed. A disease outbreak becomes far more dangerous when detection, information sharing and medical response are slow. AI could strengthen each stage of this process by analysing biological data, identifying unusual patterns, helping design medical interventions and improving logistical planning.

The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated both the value and limits of technological tools. AI methods contributed to areas such as protein analysis, drug research and outbreak modelling, but they did not replace the need for effective public [health]({{ 'health/' | relative_url }}) institutions, reliable data and international cooperation. The lesson for an AI-enabled future is that intelligence tools are most valuable when connected to trustworthy systems.

Future AI systems could contribute to a stronger global health defence network by continuously monitoring disease signals, supporting laboratories and helping researchers investigate pathogens. However, the same technologies create a security challenge because more powerful biological design tools could potentially be misused. The resilience benefit therefore depends on combining scientific capability with careful governance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undrr.org/publication/thematic-study-existential-risk-and-rapid-technological-change-advancing-risk-informed" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undrr.org">[UNDRR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undrr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thematic Study: Existential risk and rapid technological change</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thematic Study: Existential risk and rapid technological change - Advancing risk informed development &#124; UNDRR...</span></span></span>

### Making infrastructure less fragile

Modern societies rely on vast networks: electricity grids, transport systems, water supplies, communications and food [distribution]({{ 'distribution/' | relative_url }}). These systems are becoming more complex while facing pressures from climate change, population growth and extreme weather.

AI could improve resilience by helping operators predict failures, optimise energy systems, strengthen supply chains and plan responses to disasters. Analysis of AI applications in infrastructure suggests that better prediction and optimisation could reduce some future disaster-related losses, although these estimates depend on assumptions about adoption and implementation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.deloitte.com/uk/en/issues/climate/ai-for-infrastructure-resilience.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: deloitte.com">[deloitte.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">deloitte.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A I for infrastructure resilience &#124; Deloitte Global</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A I for infrastructure resilience &#124; Deloitte Global</span></span></span>

The deeper AI bloom possibility is that civilisation could become more adaptive. Instead of reacting after storms, fires or infrastructure failures occur, AI-supported systems could anticipate problems and continuously improve. A mature AI-enabled civilisation might function less like a machine that occasionally breaks and more like a living system that constantly monitors and repairs itself.

## Better coordination without authoritarian control

### Can AI help societies cooperate at global scale?

Many of humanity’s largest challenges are coordination problems. Climate change, pandemics, nuclear risks and emerging technologies cross national borders, while political systems often struggle to respond to problems that unfold over decades.

Advanced AI could help by improving forecasting, translating between languages, analysing policy choices and revealing unintended consequences. Governments, researchers and international organisations could use AI systems to understand complex trade-offs more effectively. In principle, better intelligence could help societies make decisions based on wider evidence rather than short-term pressures.

But coordination through AI raises a difficult question: who controls the systems that provide advice and influence decisions? A civilisation that becomes dependent on a small number of powerful AI providers could exchange one form of fragility for another. Concentrated ownership, surveillance capabilities or opaque decision-making could undermine the very freedoms and diversity that make civilisation resilient.

This is why AI governance is not separate from the AI bloom vision. A flourishing future requires institutions that can use powerful tools while preserving accountability, human agency and democratic choice. The debate around [frontier]({{ 'compute-controls/' | relative_url }}) AI risks includes both concerns about extreme future loss of control and concerns about more immediate problems such as misuse, concentration of power and inadequate oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">28, 2025...</span></span></span>

### Resilience requires distributed capability

A robust civilisation would not place all its knowledge, infrastructure or decision-making capacity in one location. AI could support a more [distributed]({{ 'ai-access/' | relative_url }}) future by making expertise available more widely.

For example, advanced AI tutors, scientific assistants and engineering systems could allow smaller communities and less wealthy regions to access capabilities that are currently concentrated in major institutions. This could strengthen global resilience by reducing the gap between places with abundant expertise and places without it.

However, distribution would not happen automatically. AI systems could also deepen inequality if access, computing resources and ownership remain concentrated. The question is therefore political as much as technological: whether AI becomes a shared capability or a strategic advantage controlled by a small number of actors.

## Space settlement and the expansion of conscious life

### Why leaving Earth matters in the long-term future

One of the most ambitious arguments for AI-enabled civilisation is that humanity could eventually expand beyond Earth. Today, all human life exists on one planet, making civilisation vulnerable to planet-scale events. A sufficiently advanced spacefaring civilisation would have multiple habitats and therefore reduce the risk of a single catastrophe ending the human story.

Space settlement is not a short-term solution to Earth’s problems. Building self-sustaining communities beyond Earth would require major advances in engineering, economics, biology and governance. Mars settlements, orbital habitats or other forms of space infrastructure remain difficult and uncertain.

The relevance of AI is that it could accelerate the capabilities needed for expansion: autonomous robotics, advanced materials research, life-support optimisation, scientific [discovery]({{ 'discovery/' | relative_url }}) and large-scale engineering. AI systems could operate in environments where human presence is expensive or dangerous, helping construct infrastructure before large populations arrive.

The long-term argument is not simply that humans should move to another planet. It is that intelligence may eventually allow life to become less geographically constrained. A civilisation able to spread beyond Earth would have more opportunities for discovery and survival, while also carrying greater responsibilities for how it uses that power.

## The hardest problem: avoiding a more dangerous civilisation

A future with powerful AI is not automatically a safer future. Greater capability can increase both protective capacity and destructive potential. A civilisation with advanced AI could cure diseases, improve disaster response and explore space, but it could also create new pathways for conflict, manipulation or catastrophic accidents.

This is why AI safety and [alignment]({{ 'alignment/' | relative_url }})—the challenge of ensuring advanced AI systems pursue goals compatible with human intentions and values—are central to the long-term future debate. The UK government’s discussion of frontier AI risks notes that highly capable systems could pose serious threats if they were inadequately controlled, while also recognising major uncertainty about how such scenarios might unfold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">28, 2025...</span></span></span>

There are also disagreements about which risks deserve the most attention. Some researchers emphasise possible future scenarios involving highly autonomous systems that humans cannot reliably control. Others argue that present-day issues such as misuse, inequality, political instability and weak governance deserve greater focus. A resilient approach does not need certainty about every future scenario; it requires building institutions that perform well across many possible futures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.brookings.edu/articles/are-ai-existential-risks-real-and-what-should-we-do-about-them/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brookings.edu">[brookings.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brookings.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Are AI existential risks real—and what should we do about them? &#124; Brookings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Are AI existential risks real—and what should we do about them? &#124; Brookings</span></span></span>

## What would a resilient AI future require?

An AI-enabled civilisation that genuinely supports humanity’s long future would likely need several conditions:

* **Safe [development]({{ 'build-rights/' | relative_url }}) of powerful systems:** capabilities must advance alongside methods for evaluation, control and risk reduction.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* **Broad access to benefits:** intelligence tools should improve lives globally rather than becoming concentrated sources of power.
* **Strong institutions:** governments and international organisations need the ability to manage technologies that evolve faster than traditional policy cycles.
* **Scientific openness with appropriate safeguards:** discovery should accelerate while dangerous applications are responsibly managed.
* **Long-term thinking:** societies must consider not only the next election cycle or economic quarter, but the survival and flourishing of future generations.

</div>

The central promise of AI civilisational resilience is therefore larger than protection from disaster. It is the possibility that intelligence itself becomes a tool for making civilisation more capable, more adaptable and more enduring. The long future of humanity may depend not only on reaching greater technological power, but on learning how to use that power wisely.
