## Introduction

Universal basic services (UBS) are a way of protecting living standards by guaranteeing access to essential goods and services rather than relying only on wages or cash payments. In an economy shaped by advanced AI and [automation]({{ 'labour-share/' | relative_url }}), the idea matters because even a society that becomes much richer could still leave people insecure if housing, [healthcare]({{ 'health-algorithms/' | relative_url }}), education, transport and other necessities remain expensive or difficult to access. UBS aims to create a stable foundation: people would have the essentials needed to participate in society even if employment becomes less predictable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucl.ac.uk/bartlett/research-projects/2022/jan/universal-basic-services" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucl.ac.uk">[ucl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Universal Basic Services &#124; UCL Bartlett Faculty of the Built Environment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 23, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 23, 2022</span></span></span>

The strongest case for UBS in an AI-enabled future is not that it replaces all income or solves every economic problem. Rather, it changes the relationship between people and essential costs. If AI increases productivity and lowers the cost of producing many goods and services, universal access systems could help ensure that those gains translate into everyday security instead of simply higher returns for those who own the technology. But the model also raises difficult questions: which services should be guaranteed, how much choice individuals should retain, how systems should be funded, and how governments can avoid creating slow or unresponsive public monopolies.

## How essential services reduce financial pressure

The central mechanism behind universal basic services is simple: households need less income to achieve a secure standard of living when some essential needs are provided collectively.

A person who receives healthcare, [education]({{ 'education/' | relative_url }}), affordable housing support, childcare, transport access or digital connectivity through public systems does not need to purchase all of these separately from their wages. This can act as a form of “social income” — not money paid directly into an account, but real value delivered through reduced costs and greater security. The UCL Institute for Global Prosperity describes UBS as services provided free at the point of need that expand people’s opportunities, participation and capabilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucl.ac.uk/bartlett/research-projects/2022/jan/universal-basic-services" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucl.ac.uk">[ucl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Universal Basic Services &#124; UCL Bartlett Faculty of the Built Environment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 23, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 23, 2022</span></span></span>

This becomes especially relevant if employment patterns change. AI may not immediately eliminate most jobs, and current evidence suggests automation is developing unevenly across occupations. However, even without mass unemployment, workers may face more frequent transitions between jobs, training, caring responsibilities and different forms of employment. A system that guarantees essential services reduces the risk that every transition becomes a personal financial crisis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oecd.org/en/about/projects/ai-and-the-future-of-social-protection.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oecd.org">[oecd.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oecd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A I and the Future of Social Protection &#124; OECD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A I and the Future of Social Protection &#124; OECD</span></span></span>

The practical effect is different from a traditional wage-based model:

* **Affordable education and training** make career changes easier when technology changes demand for skills.
* **Housing support** reduces exposure to one of the largest household expenses.

Universal healthcare provides a clear example of the mechanism. The World [Health]({{ 'health/' | relative_url }}) Organization defines universal health coverage as ensuring people can access needed health services without financial hardship. Yet global evidence shows why financial protection matters: millions of people still experience serious economic strain from paying directly for healthcare.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/universal-health-coverage-%28uhc%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: who.int">[World Health Organization]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">who.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Health Organization Universal health coverage (UHC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Health OrganizationUniversal health coverage (UHC)December 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 5, 2025</span></span></span>

In an AI bloom scenario, the argument for UBS becomes broader. If technology eventually creates far greater abundance, the question shifts from “can society produce enough?” to “can people reliably access what society can produce?” UBS is one possible institutional answer to that second question.

## Which needs public systems can cover

Universal basic services do not usually mean that every possible good becomes free. The focus is on foundational services that strongly affect [human]({{ 'human-feedback/' | relative_url }}) capability and participation.

### Health and care

Healthcare is often considered the clearest example because access affects both human wellbeing and economic security. A person who can receive treatment early is more able to work, learn, care for others and participate socially. Without protection from large medical costs, even high-income societies can leave people vulnerable to sudden shocks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.who.int/health-topics/financial-protection" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: who.int">[World Health Organization]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">who.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Health Organization Financial protection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Health Organization Financial protection</span></span></span>

Future AI advances could make this area even more important. AI-assisted diagnosis, drug discovery and personalised medicine could expand healthcare capability, but the benefits depend on whether access is broadly distributed. A future where AI accelerates medical breakthroughs but only wealthy groups can afford advanced care would fall short of the wider human flourishing goals associated with AI abundance.

### Education and lifelong learning

In an economy where AI changes tasks quickly, education becomes less like a one-time investment and more like a lifelong public capability.

Universal access to high-quality learning could help people use AI tools effectively, move into new fields and develop uniquely human abilities such as judgement, creativity and collaboration. Public education systems already provide much of this foundation, but an AI-driven economy may increase demand for continuous retraining and personalised learning support.

### Housing and basic living conditions

Housing is one of the most difficult areas for UBS because governments have less direct control over supply, land prices and construction costs. However, housing support, social housing and planning reforms can reduce the share of income required simply to remain secure.

This matters because a society can experience technological abundance while households remain under pressure from fixed costs. Lower costs for information, software or manufactured goods do not automatically solve expensive housing markets.

### Transport and digital access

Transport and internet access are increasingly viewed as enabling services because they determine whether people can participate in modern economic and social life.

A person without reliable connectivity may struggle to access education, healthcare appointments, government services or AI-enabled tools. As more public services become digital, access to technology itself becomes part of social infrastructure rather than merely a consumer choice.

## Why services may matter in an AI-rich economy

Universal [basic income]({{ 'basic-income/' | relative_url }}) (UBI) and universal basic services are often compared because both attempt to provide security when employment becomes less central. They solve different problems.

UBI provides flexibility: people receive money and decide how to spend it. UBS provides guaranteed access: society ensures that certain needs are met regardless of individual purchasing power.

The distinction matters because some needs cannot be solved easily through cash alone. If housing supply is limited, additional income can increase competition for homes rather than guarantee affordability. If healthcare prices rise faster than incomes, cash payments may not provide the same security as a functioning public health system.

Supporters of UBS argue that shared systems can also create efficiency by pooling resources. The economist Ian Gough has argued that UBS is based on the idea that some human needs are best met through collective systems rather than individual purchasing decisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/101051/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.lse.ac.uk">[eprints.lse.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.lse.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Universal Basic Services: a theoretical and moral framework</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LSE Research Online...</span></span></span>

However, UBS is not automatically superior. Cash provides personal choice, and public systems can become bureaucratic or inflexible. A future society may need a combination: universal services for essential foundations, income support for personal freedom, and labour-market policies that help people adapt to technological change.

## The trade-off between security and personal choice

The biggest debate around universal basic services is where to draw the boundary between collective provision and individual choice.

A strong UBS system can provide stability. People may be more willing to change careers, start businesses, care for relatives or pursue education if losing a job does not threaten access to essentials. In this sense, security can increase freedom rather than reduce it.

But critics raise several concerns.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Choice:** A public service may not meet everyone’s preferences. Some people may prefer private alternatives or different models of provision.
* **Cost:** High-quality universal services require sustainable taxation and effective administration.
* **Capacity:** Governments must ensure that services expand when demand grows.
* **Innovation:** Poorly designed systems could become resistant to improvement.

</div>

The challenge is therefore not simply whether services should exist, but how they should be designed. A successful UBS model would need accountability, user choice where possible, transparent performance standards and continuous improvement.

AI itself could affect this debate. The OECD notes that AI has potential to improve social protection systems by making services easier to access, identifying needs earlier and reducing administrative burdens, while also warning about risks such as poor decisions and loss of human oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oecd.org/en/about/projects/ai-and-the-future-of-social-protection.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oecd.org">[oecd.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oecd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A I and the Future of Social Protection &#124; OECD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A I and the Future of Social Protection &#124; OECD</span></span></span>

## What universal services would need to achieve for AI bloom

For the optimistic AI future to become a shared human future, abundance must translate into capability. People need more than access to cheap products; they need the foundations that allow them to learn, create, stay healthy and participate.

Universal basic services represent one possible bridge between technological progress and everyday security. They do not guarantee equality, and they cannot replace good governance, democratic accountability or fair [distribution]({{ 'distribution/' | relative_url }}) of AI-generated wealth. But they address a central problem: even a highly productive civilisation can fail to deliver human flourishing if essential needs remain insecure.

The long-term question for an AI-enabled society is therefore not only how much intelligence machines create, but how intelligently societies organise access to the benefits that intelligence makes possible. UBS is one mechanism for ensuring that greater technological capability becomes a wider expansion of human opportunity rather than simply a larger economy with the same vulnerabilities.
